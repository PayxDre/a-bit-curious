Nature of Money

The foundation of everything that follows · ~5 min read

What is money, really? The six properties of sound money, why gold won for 5,000 years, and the test every currency must pass. This is the reading version - the interactive lesson has the live demos, quizzes, and progress tracking.

⚠️ NOTICE: The dollar has lost 97% of its purchasing power since 1913. Your government calls this "price stability." Read the next few sections and decide for yourself what to call it.

Money is the single greatest tool for human cooperation ever invented. Before money existed, people had to barter - swapping goods directly. Imagine trying to pay your dentist with a chicken. Money solved this by becoming a universal medium of exchange: instead of trading things for things, you trade things for money, then trade money for anything else you need. But money is also a device for storing the value of your time and labor. Every dollar you earn represents hours of your life you can never get back. Money allows you to store that labor and deploy it in the future - to save for retirement, build a business, or leave something for your children. A civilization without sound money is a civilization that cannot cooperate, cannot plan, and cannot build long-term prosperity.

Throughout human history, many different things have served as money. Cowrie shells were used in Africa and Asia for centuries. Glass beads, wampum belts, and decorated stones served similar roles in various cultures. Salt was so valuable in ancient Rome that soldiers were sometimes paid in it - the origin of the word "salary." Cattle and livestock served as money across most of the ancient world. Eventually, societies converged on precious metals - particularly gold and silver - as the dominant monetary medium. This convergence happened independently across cultures that had no contact with each other: Mesopotamia, China, Greece, Rome, the Aztec Empire. The world kept reinventing gold-based money because gold has properties nothing else could match.

Every form of money that has ever existed has been tested against one brutal, simple question: can someone else easily create more of it? If the answer is yes, that money eventually fails. Cowrie shells seemed scarce until Europeans arrived with ships and dumped millions of them on African markets, destroying the purchasing power of indigenous wealth overnight. Tobacco was used as money in colonial Virginia until overproduction collapsed its value. Rai stones from the island of Yap seemed impossible to counterfeit - until a single outsider with a boat broke the system. The consistent lesson of monetary history is this: good money must be scarce by nature, durable, divisible, portable, and recognizable. Most historical monies failed on at least one of these dimensions.

Gold came closest to ideal money and reigned for millennia - but it has critical weaknesses. It is heavy and difficult to transport. It is hard to divide precisely - you cannot easily send someone 0.000001 oz of gold. It is not instantly verifiable - is that coin real gold or gold-plated lead? Crucially, gold led inexorably to centralization. Governments and banks took custody of everyone's gold, issued paper receipts, then quietly printed more receipts than they had gold. When people eventually tried to redeem their paper for real gold - as in 1933 in the USA, when FDR confiscated private gold - the game was up. By 1971, Nixon closed the gold window entirely, severing the last link between paper money and physical gold. Humanity's 5,000-year experiment with gold-backed money ended with a unilateral government decree.

What replaced gold? Fiat currency - money created by government decree, backed by nothing but promises and enforced by law. The US dollar, the euro, the yen, the pound: every currency on Earth today is fiat money. There is not an ounce of gold, silver, or any other physical asset backing any of them. They exist because governments say they exist, and you accept them because the law requires you to, and because everyone around you does the same. Since 1971, the US dollar has lost over 85% of its purchasing power. A dollar saved in 1971 is worth approximately 13 cents in real terms today. Since the Federal Reserve was created in 1913, the loss is over 97%.

A dollar saved in 1913 buys 3 cents worth of goods today. The other 97 cents were stolen by inflation.

🚨 The Brutal Truth: Fiat Money Is Legalized Theft Every dollar created from thin air dilutes every dollar you already hold. The fiat system doesn't just erode purchasing power - it rewires human behavior. When money loses value over time, there is no incentive to save. People borrow and consume instead. They take on debt. They speculate in asset bubbles. The boom-bust cycle that devastates ordinary families every decade is not a bug in the fiat system - it is a feature.

But how deep does the deception go?

You've seen the purchasing power loss. Now let's look at how the system actually works - the mechanics of money creation, the manipulation of inflation statistics, and the invisible tax on your savings.

Key terms

barter
Barter = trade goods directly. "I'll give you five fish if you fix my roof." The problem: what if the roofer doesn't want fish? Economists call this the "double coincidence of wants" problem - both parties must want exactly what the other has. Massively inefficient.
time and labor
When you work for 8 hours, you are converting your irreplaceable time - the hours of your life - into money. Money is literally crystallized human time and energy. This is why inflation, which silently erodes money's value, is not merely an economic phenomenon. It is time theft.
Cowrie shells
Used for thousands of years across Africa, Asia, and the Americas. Durable, portable, and beautiful. But fatally easy to find near any ocean - meaning anyone with a bucket and a boat could "create" more money, destroying its value instantly.
precious metals
Gold and silver became the dominant forms of money for most of recorded history because they had the ideal properties: rare (hard to mine), durable (don't rust or rot), divisible (can be melted and recast), and recognizable (hard to fake). For 5,000 years, gold was the global reserve asset.
Rai stones
Enormous limestone discs used as money on the Pacific island of Yap. They worked well - until an Irish-American adventurer named David O'Keefe figured out he could import them by ship in bulk, massively inflating the supply and wrecking the monetary system.
scarce by nature
Scarcity cannot be granted by a king, a central bank, or a constitution. It must be an intrinsic property of the money itself - like gold's rarity in the Earth's crust, or Bitcoin's hard-coded 21-million cap. Any money whose supply can be expanded by human decision will eventually be expanded - and debased.
heavy and difficult to transport
A million dollars in gold weighs about 50 pounds and fills a briefcase. Try paying for a house, a ship, or an army with it. Throughout history, transporting gold required armored wagons, warships, and armed guards. This is extraordinarily impractical for a modern global economy.
centralization
Because gold is hard to transport and store, people naturally deposited it with trusted institutions - first goldsmiths, then banks. Banks issued "receipts" (early paper money) redeemable for gold. But banks quickly realized: most depositors never claimed their gold at once. So they started issuing MORE receipts than they had gold. Fractional reserve banking - and the seed of every monetary crisis in modern history - was born.
closed the gold window
On August 15, 1971, President Nixon announced the US dollar would no longer be redeemable for gold at the fixed rate of $35/oz. This ended the Bretton Woods system and untethered every currency on Earth from physical reality. Since that day, every national currency in the world is backed by nothing but government promises.
Fiat currency
"Fiat" is Latin for "let it be done" or "by decree." Fiat money has value because a government declares it has value and forces you to accept it for taxes and legal contracts (called "legal tender" laws). It is backed by nothing except trust in the government that issues it.
85% of its purchasing power
In 1971, a dollar could buy roughly what $7.80 buys today - an 87% decline. Since the Federal Reserve was created in 1913, the dollar has lost 97% of its purchasing power: what cost $1 then costs over $31 today.
borrow and consume
In a world of low interest rates and perpetual inflation, the rational strategy is to borrow as much as possible, buy assets (houses, stocks, gold), and let inflation erode your debt while assets rise in price. This punishes the frugal and rewards the reckless.
boom-bust cycle
Central banks lower interest rates → cheap credit floods the economy → speculation and malinvestment boom → bubble inflates → central bank raises rates or the bubble naturally pops → recession → repeat forever.
mathematically enforced supply cap
21 million Bitcoin. Not because a CEO decided it. Not because a government decreed it. Because it is written into the code that runs on every node in the world simultaneously. No single person, company, government, or army can change it.
hardest money ever created
"Hard money" means money that is hard to produce more of. Gold was hard money. Bitcoin is the hardest money ever created: after the year 2140, no new Bitcoin will ever be created. Its stock-to-flow ratio already surpasses gold and rises with every halving.
