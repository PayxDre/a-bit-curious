⚠️ Case Study: The Coldcard Entropy Bug (July 2026)
Bitcoin worked perfectly. The dice did not.[46]

In late July 2026, Coinkite disclosed that a bug in Coldcard firmware had been quietly crippling seed generation since March 2021. Inside libngu, the device's cryptography library, a preprocessor check tested whether a configuration macro was defined rather than whether it was actually switched on. Every build from firmware 4.0.1 onward silently fell back to Yasmarang - a fast, non-cryptographic pseudo-random generator borrowed from MicroPython - instead of the STM32 chip's hardware true random number generator. Worse, that fallback was seeded once from the chip's unique ID and timer registers, then never fed fresh randomness again.

A 24-word seed is supposed to carry 128 bits of entropy - more possible keys than there are atoms in our galaxy, which is the entire reason nobody can guess yours. On affected devices, real entropy collapsed to roughly 72 bits on Mk4, Q and Mk5, and roughly 40 bits on Mk2 and Mk3. Forty bits is about a trillion possibilities. That is not a universe. That is a weekend and a decent graphics card.

Someone found it. Beginning around 01:10 UTC on July 30, 2026, coins started leaving wallets whose owners had done nothing wrong that morning - no phishing link, no leaked backup, no malware. Galaxy Research went on to map three waves of sweeps through August 1, totalling roughly 1,367 BTC (about $88.6 million) across 4,585 addresses. WHO GOT SWEPT
Seeds generated on the device itself on firmware 4.0.1 or later, held in a single-signature wallet, with no passphrase and no dice rolls added. They were guessable from the moment they were created - the owners just did not know it for five years.
WHO WAS FINE
Anyone who mixed in 50+ of their own dice rolls or used a strong, unique BIP-39 passphrase. Also safe: seeds made before v4.0.0, the Mk1, Tapsigner, Opendime and Satscard, and multisig quorums whose other keys came from a different vendor.
🔧 IF YOU OWN A COLDCARD
1. Update to patched firmware - Mk3 to 4.2.0, Mk4 and Mk5 to 5.6.0, Q to 1.5.0Q (Edge builds: 6.6.0X and 6.6.0QX).
2. Understand that updating does not repair a seed that was already made weakly. The damage is baked into the words.
3. Generate a brand new seed on the patched firmware and move your coins to it, treating the old wallet as compromised.
4. Add your own dice rolls while you do it, and write the new words down before you send a single satoshi.

Here is the uncomfortable part: the protocol never wavered. No consensus rule broke, no node was fooled, and every one of those thefts was a perfectly valid Bitcoin transaction signed by a key the attacker had legitimately guessed. Bitcoin will faithfully execute a theft if someone can produce your key - and weak randomness hands that key over quietly, years in advance. "Don't trust, verify" is not only about the ledger. It applies to the box that generates your keys too, which is exactly why rolling your own dice and spreading a multisig across different vendors are worth the extra ten minutes.

Money is the single greatest tool for human cooperation ever invented. Before money existed, people had to barterBarter = trade goods directly. "I'll give you five fish if you fix my roof." The problem: what if the roofer doesn't want fish? Economists call this the "double coincidence of wants" problem - both parties must want exactly what the other has. Massively inefficient. - swapping goods directly. Imagine trying to pay your dentist with a chicken. Money solved this by becoming a universal medium of exchange: instead of trading things for things, you trade things for money, then trade money for anything else you need. But money is also a device for storing the value of your time and laborWhen you work for 8 hours, you are converting your irreplaceable time - the hours of your life - into money. Money is literally crystallized human time and energy. This is why inflation, which silently erodes money's value, is not merely an economic phenomenon. It is time theft.. Every dollar you earn represents hours of your life you can never get back. Money allows you to store that labor and deploy it in the future - to save for retirement, build a business, or leave something for your children. A civilization without sound money is a civilization that cannot cooperate, cannot plan, and cannot build long-term prosperity.

Throughout human history, many different things have served as money. Cowrie shellsUsed for thousands of years across Africa, Asia, and the Americas. Durable, portable, and beautiful. But fatally easy to find near any ocean - meaning anyone with a bucket and a boat could "create" more money, destroying its value instantly. were used in Africa and Asia for centuries. Eventually, societies converged on precious metalsGold and silver became the dominant forms of money for most of recorded history because they had the ideal properties: rare (hard to mine), durable (don't rust or rot), divisible (can be melted and recast), and recognizable (hard to fake). For 5,000 years, gold was the global reserve asset. - particularly gold and silver - as the dominant monetary medium. This convergence happened independently across cultures that had no contact with each other: Mesopotamia, China, Greece, Rome, the Aztec Empire. The world kept reinventing gold-based money because gold has properties nothing else could match.

Every form of money that has ever existed has been tested against one brutal, simple question: can someone else easily create more of it? If the answer is yes, that money eventually fails. Gold came closest to ideal money and reigned for millennia - but it has critical weaknesses. Why does consensus matter?

Proof of Work and Proof of Stake determine who controls the network and how power is distributed over time.

⚒️ Proof of Work vs Proof of Stake

// How consensus mechanisms shape who has power
After this lesson you can explainwhy Bitcoin secures itself with energy rather than deposits.

Every blockchain needs a way to decide which transactions are valid and who gets to add the next block. This mechanism - called consensus - determines who holds power over the network, how that power is distributed, and whether it concentrates over time. Bitcoin uses Proof of Work. Most other major cryptocurrencies have moved to Proof of Stake. The differences are fundamental.

⚒️ Proof of Work (PoW)

In Proof of Work, miners compete to solve a computationally difficult puzzle. The first to find a valid solution gets to propose the next block and earns the block reward. This process requires real-world resources - specialized hardware (ASICs) and electricity - creating an external cost that cannot be faked or shortcut.

Open participation
Anyone with hardware and electricity can mine. No permission needed, no minimum balance required. A miner in Nigeria competes on equal terms with a miner in Texas.
Real-world anchor
Security is backed by physics, not financial promises. To attack Bitcoin, you'd need to outspend the entire network's energy output - currently billions of dollars per year in electricity alone.
Wealth does not equal control
Holding a large amount of bitcoin does not give you any influence over block production or network rules. Mining power comes from ongoing operational expenditure, not from accumulated wealth.
Proven track record
Bitcoin's Proof of Work has secured the network for 17 years with zero downtime, zero rollbacks, and zero successful attacks. No other consensus mechanism has this track record.
💰 Proof of Stake (PoS)

In Proof of Stake, validators are selected to propose blocks based on how many tokens they hold and "stake" as collateral. The more tokens you lock up, the more likely you are to be chosen to validate the next block - and the more rewards you earn. There is no external cost like electricity; validation requires only holding the token itself.

Wealth concentration
Validators with larger stakes earn proportionally more rewards, which they can re-stake to earn even more. Over time, the largest holders grow their share of the network, while smaller participants fall further behind. The rich get richer by design.
Governance power tied to wealth
In PoS systems, the entities with the most tokens have the most influence over which transactions are validated and which blocks are accepted. Holding a large balance directly translates into control over what the network considers valid.
No external cost
Since validation doesn't require energy expenditure, the cost of attacking a PoS network is purely financial - buy enough tokens and you control the chain. In PoW, you'd also need physical infrastructure and ongoing electricity, which can't be acquired instantly.
Censorship risk
Large PoS validators are identifiable entities with known holdings. Governments can pressure them to censor specific transactions or addresses. After Ethereum's move to PoS, research showed that a majority of blocks were being produced by validators running OFAC-compliant relay software, filtering certain transactions.
📈 The compounding problem: In Proof of Stake, staking rewards function like interest - your tokens generate more tokens. A validator staking 1,000,000 ETH earns proportionally more than someone staking 1,000 ETH. Those rewards are then re-staked, earning even more. Over time, this creates a compounding effect where the largest holders capture an ever-growing share of the total supply. This dynamic mirrors the traditional financial system, where those with the most capital earn the highest returns and accumulate wealth at a faster rate than smaller participants.

🔄 Why Proof of Work distributes wealth and Proof of Stake concentrates it

Bitcoin miners have significant ongoing costs - electricity, cooling, hardware replacement, facility leases. To cover these expenses, miners must regularly sell a portion of their bitcoin earnings on the open market. This creates constant sell pressure that distributes newly mined coins into circulation. The coins flow outward, from miners to the broader economy.

Proof of Stake validators, by contrast, have minimal operating costs. Their primary "expense" is the opportunity cost of locking tokens. Since they don't need to sell to cover real-world bills, they can re-stake 100% of their rewards, compounding their position. A validator staking 10% of the total supply today will hold an even larger percentage next year, and an even larger one the year after that - without doing any additional work.

This is the same dynamic that exists in traditional finance: capital generates returns, returns are reinvested, and wealth concentrates at the top. Proof of Work breaks this cycle by requiring ongoing real-world expenditure. Proof of Stake replicates it.

🔒 Censorship in practice: the OFAC example

After Ethereum transitioned to Proof of Stake in September 2022, researchers began tracking how many blocks were produced by validators running MEV-Boost relays that comply with U.S. Treasury OFAC sanctions. At its peak, over 70% of Ethereum blocks were being built by OFAC-compliant relays - meaning transactions involving sanctioned addresses were being excluded from the majority of blocks.

This happened because large PoS validators - companies like Coinbase, Lido, and Kraken - are identifiable, regulated entities. When the U.S. government sanctioned Tornado Cash (a privacy tool) in August 2022, these validators had strong incentives to comply. Their staked capital was at risk if they were deemed non-compliant.

In Bitcoin's Proof of Work system, miners are more geographically distributed and mining operations can be relocated. When China banned Bitcoin mining in 2021, hashrate moved to the U.S., Kazakhstan, Russia, and elsewhere within months. The network never went down and no transactions were censored. The physical nature of mining equipment makes it harder - though not impossible - for any single government to enforce transaction-level censorship across the network.

⚠️ The "nothing at stake" problem

In Proof of Work, mining on a competing fork costs real electricity. Miners must choose which chain to dedicate their hardware to, because they can't mine both simultaneously without splitting their hashrate. This economic cost enforces a natural commitment to one chain.

In Proof of Stake, validators can theoretically sign blocks on multiple competing forks at zero additional cost - since there's no energy expenditure per signature. This is known as the "nothing at stake" problem. PoS systems attempt to address this through "slashing" penalties (destroying a validator's stake if they're caught signing competing blocks), but these mechanisms add complexity and rely on the correct functioning of the penalty system itself. Proof of Work solves this problem through physics: you can't use the same electricity twice.

The core tradeoff is straightforward. Proof of Stake is more energy-efficient - that is its primary advantage. But in exchange, it creates a system where the wealthiest participants control validation, earn the most rewards, and grow their influence over time. Proof of Work requires real-world resources, which means mining power is earned through ongoing expenditure rather than accumulated through existing wealth. Bitcoin's design choice to use Proof of Work reflects its core purpose: a monetary system where no one gets special privileges based on how much they already hold.

VECTOR #3 Double Spend - Spend the Same Coin Twice

What if someone sends Bitcoin to a merchant and then quickly sends the same coins elsewhere before the first transaction confirms?

Confirmations solve this. Once a transaction is included in a block (1 confirmation), reversing it requires outpacing the entire network. After 6 confirmations (~60 minutes), the probability of reversal is effectively zero - you'd need more computing power than exists on Earth. For small purchases, even 1 confirmation is sufficient. For large transfers, waiting for 6 is standard. The deeper a transaction is buried in the chain, the more energy it would cost to undo - exponentially.

VECTOR #4 Nation-State Attack - Government Tries to Kill Bitcoin

Could a government with unlimited resources shut Bitcoin down?

They'd have to shut down the internet - globally, simultaneously, forever. Bitcoin nodes run in 100+ countries. The protocol can transmit over satellite (Blockstream Satellite), mesh radio, and even SMS. China - the most powerful censorship apparatus in history - banned Bitcoin mining twice. The hash rate recovered in months as miners relocated. The network didn't skip a beat. A government can ban exchanges within its borders, but it cannot stop two people from sending math to each other over the internet.

VECTOR #5 Software Bug - A Flaw in the Code

Bitcoin is software. What if there's a critical bug?

Bitcoin's code is the most scrutinized open-source project in history. Hundreds of developers review every line. There have been bugs - in 2010, an overflow bug briefly allowed someone to create 184 billion Bitcoin, which was patched within hours. But here's the key: Bitcoin's consensus mechanism means every node independently validates every transaction. Even if one version of the software has a bug, the rest of the network rejects invalid blocks. There is no single point of failure. No "main server" to hack. No CEO to coerce. The code is public, the network is distributed, and anyone can audit it.

VECTOR #6 Eclipse Attack - Isolate a Single Node

Instead of attacking the whole network, what if you surround a single node with attacker-controlled nodes, feeding it false information?

This targets individuals, not the network itself. Even if successful, it only affects the isolated node - the rest of the network is unaffected. Bitcoin Core has built-in defenses: diversified peer connections, anchor connections, and limits on connections from the same IP range. Running your own node with multiple connection sources makes this attack extremely difficult. And it still can't create fake Bitcoin or alter the chain - it can only delay your view of new blocks.

VECTOR #7 Social Attack - Change the Rules from the Inside

What if powerful companies or influential developers try to change Bitcoin's rules - like raising the 21 million cap?

This was tried in 2017 - and it failed spectacularly. Major companies, exchanges, and miners backed the "SegWit2x" proposal to increase block size. They had overwhelming corporate support. But Bitcoin's rules are enforced by node operators - the thousands of individuals running the software. The nodes rejected the change. The corporate-backed fork died. This proved something remarkable: no one controls Bitcoin. Not the miners, not the developers, not the corporations. The users - through their nodes - have the final say.

🛡 Why Bitcoin Is Antifragile
Every attack makes the network stronger. Here's why the security model works.
💰 Economic Incentives Attacking costs more than cooperating
⚡ Thermodynamic Security Real energy = real cost to attack
🌐 Global Distribution No single point of failure, 100+ countries
👀 Radical Transparency Open source code, public ledger, anyone can audit
👥 User Sovereignty Node operators enforce the rules, not corporations
Bitcoin turns the attacker's own resources against them. The bigger the attack, the more expensive it gets, the less it accomplishes, and the stronger the network becomes afterward.

This is why Bitcoin is often called antifragile. Unlike traditional systems that get weaker under stress, Bitcoin gets stronger. Every attack attempt, every ban, every crash - the network adapts, the community hardens, and the protocol keeps producing blocks. Every 10 minutes. Without fail. For 17 years and counting.

Even an attacker with most of the mining power can only reverse their own recent payments, at enormous cost. They cannot spend your coins or create new ones, because every node rejects invalid blocks.

⚖️ Consensus & Upgrades

// How Bitcoin evolves without a CEO
After this lesson you can explainhow Bitcoin changes when nobody is in charge.

Bitcoin has no CEO, no board of directors, no foundation with veto power. Yet its code has been upgraded dozens of times since 2009. How? Through a process called rough consensus - a messy, slow, beautiful system where changes only happen when nearly everyone agrees.

Most tech companies push updates overnight. Bitcoin's upgrade process is deliberately slow and conservative. That's not a bug - it's what makes it trustworthy as a monetary protocol.

📜 The BIP Process - How Changes Are Proposed

BIP = Bitcoin Improvement Proposal. Anyone in the world can submit one. Here's how it works:

1
Draft & Discuss
A developer writes a formal proposal (BIP) describing the change, its motivation, and technical specification. It gets posted to the bitcoin-dev mailing list and debated publicly. Anyone can participate.
2
Code & Review
If the idea gains traction, developers write the actual code. Multiple independent reviewers audit it. This phase can take months or years. There's no deadline - the code ships when it's ready.
3
Signal & Activate
For consensus changes, miners signal readiness by including a flag in the blocks they mine. When a supermajority (usually 90-95%) signals support over a defined period, the upgrade activates. Nodes that haven't upgraded still work - that's the beauty of soft forks.

Think of a soft fork as adding a speed bump to a road - all cars still work. A hard fork is changing the road to train tracks - cars can't drive on them.

Bitcoin strongly favors soft forks because they don't force anyone to upgrade. If you're running a node from 2017, it still validates the chain today. That's 9 years of backwards compatibility - unheard of in software.

⚖️ The Blocksize War (2015-2017)
SegWit didn't happen easily. It emerged from a bitter 2-year civil war over how to scale Bitcoin. One side (mostly large companies and miners) wanted to increase the block size via hard fork. The other side (mostly developers and node operators) pushed SegWit as a soft fork. The conflict peaked with the SegWit2x proposal - a backroom deal by major companies to force both SegWit and a 2MB hard fork. Users revolted. They ran nodes signaling "NO2X." SegWit2x collapsed weeks before activation. The lesson: in Bitcoin, users - not companies - have the final say.

2021
🌱 Taproot (BIPs 340-342) - Schnorr & MAST
Bitcoin's most recent major upgrade, activated November 2021. Taproot introduced three powerful changes:
Schnorr Signatures (BIP 340)
Replaced ECDSA for Taproot outputs. Schnorr signatures are smaller, faster to verify, and enable signature aggregation - combining multiple signatures into one. A 3-of-5 multisig transaction becomes indistinguishable from a simple payment.
MAST (Merkelized Alternative Script Trees)
Allows complex spending conditions (like "Alice after 1 year OR Bob+Carol together") to be hidden in a Merkle tree. Only the condition actually used gets revealed on-chain. Better privacy, lower fees.
Tapscript (BIP 342)
An upgraded scripting language that makes future soft forks easier to deploy. Think of it as future-proofing Bitcoin's programming language.
Taproot was activated via "Speedy Trial" - miners signaled 90% support within a single difficulty period. Notably non-contentious compared to SegWit. Soft fork.
🔥 Current & Proposed Changes (2024-2026)

These proposals are actively debated. None are activated. This is consensus in action - the slow, careful process working as designed.

OP_CAT (BIP 347)
Would re-enable the ability to concatenate (join) two pieces of data in Bitcoin's scripting language. Satoshi originally included OP_CAT but disabled it in 2010 as a precaution. Proponents say it enables covenants, vaults, and bridges to other chains. Critics worry about unintended consequences and complexity creep. Status: actively debated.
OP_CTV - CheckTemplateVerify (BIP 119)
Would allow a transaction to commit to the exact shape of future transactions that spend it. Enables "covenants" - restrictions on how bitcoin can be spent in the future. Use cases include vaults (delayed withdrawals for security) and congestion control. Status: proposed since 2020, debated.
OP_VAULT (BIP 345)
A specialized covenant design for Bitcoin vaults - a security feature where you can "unvault" bitcoin with a time delay, giving you a window to claw it back if your keys are compromised. Like a safe with a time lock and a panic button. Status: draft proposal.
Great Script Restoration
A proposal by Rusty Russell to re-enable many of the original opcodes Satoshi disabled in 2010, plus add new ones. The idea: give Bitcoin a more expressive scripting language for building advanced contracts, layer 2 protocols, and vaults. Controversial because it significantly expands Bitcoin's attack surface. Status: early discussion.
🔥 BIP 110 - Temporarily Limit Arbitrary Data
The most contentious Bitcoin debate since the 2017 Blocksize War. Proposed in late 2025 by developer Dathon Ohm and backed by the Bitcoin Knots team, BIP 110 would impose a one-year soft fork limiting non-monetary data in transactions - targeting Ordinals inscriptions, BRC-20 tokens, and large OP_RETURN payloads. Outputs would be capped at 34 bytes, OP_RETURN at 83 bytes.
FOR: "Clean up spam"
Arbitrary data bloats the blockchain, drives up fees for real payments, and burdens node operators. Bitcoin should prioritize its monetary function.
AGAINST: "Dangerous precedent"
Consensus-level content filtering violates Bitcoin's neutrality. It disables OP_SUCCESS (needed for future upgrades) and could set a precedent for transaction censorship. Adam Back and Michael Saylor have both warned against it.
The debate echoes the 2017 war: who decides what Bitcoin is "for"? Ocean pool mined the first BIP-110 signaling block in early 2026. As of now, ~2.4% of nodes support it - far from the activation threshold, but the ideological rift runs deep. Status: actively contentious, signaling underway.
The fact that these proposals take years to debate is a feature, not a bug. Bitcoin's monetary policy is settled. Everything else moves carefully.

Here's what makes Bitcoin's governance unique: no single entity can push through a change. Not the developers (they write code, but can't force anyone to run it). Not the miners (they process transactions, but users choose which chain to follow). Not the exchanges (they list coins, but can't change the protocol). Power is distributed across all participants - and that distribution is what makes Bitcoin's monetary properties trustworthy.

Compare this to every other monetary system in history. The Federal Reserve can change interest rates in an afternoon. A government can freeze bank accounts overnight. Even Ethereum's leadership pushed through a chain rollback in 2016 (the DAO fork). Bitcoin's upgrade process is slow and frustrating by design - because the alternative is a monetary system that can be changed on a whim.

💬 Explain it to a friend

Anyone can propose a change, and nobody can impose one. Upgrades spread only when the people running nodes choose to adopt them, which makes Bitcoin slow to change and very hard to capture.

⚡ The Lightning Network

// Bitcoin's instant payment layer · ~4 min read
After this lesson you can explainhow Bitcoin can handle everyday payments.

Bitcoin's base layer is intentionally slow. It processes about 7 transactions per second - by design. That's the tradeoff for maximum decentralization and security. But you don't need to broadcast every coffee purchase to 50,000 nodes worldwide. That's where the Lightning Network comes in.

Lightning is Bitcoin's Layer 2 - a payment network built on top of Bitcoin. Think of it like this: Layer 1 (the blockchain) is the settlement layer - like a central bank clearing large transfers. Layer 2 (Lightning) is the payment layer - like swiping a debit card for everyday purchases. Both use real BTC. There is no separate "Lightning coin."

⚡ How Lightning Works - In 4 Steps
1
Open a Channel
Alice and Bob each lock some BTC into a shared 2-of-2 multisig wallet on the blockchain. This is the only on-chain transaction needed. Think of it like putting cash into a joint tab at a bar.
2
Transact Instantly, Off-Chain
Now Alice and Bob can send BTC back and forth unlimited times - instantly, for nearly zero fees. Each payment updates their balance in the channel. None of these transactions touch the blockchain. It's like passing IOUs back and forth on the tab.
3
Route Through the Network
Alice doesn't need a direct channel with everyone. If Alice has a channel with Bob, and Bob has one with Carol, Alice can pay Carol by routing through Bob - using Hashed Timelock Contracts (HTLCs)[39] that make the payment trustless. No one can steal funds in transit. It works like internet routing - data finds a path.
4
Close the Channel & Settle
When they're done, either party closes the channel. One final on-chain transaction records the net result of all their Lightning payments. Hundreds or thousands of payments get compressed into a single blockchain entry.

Lightning also uses onion routing - the same privacy concept as Tor. Each node in the payment path only knows the previous and next hop, never the full route. This gives Lightning payments better privacy than on-chain transactions in many cases.

The key insight: Bitcoin doesn't need to compete with Visa at Layer 1. It needs to be more secure than any vault at Layer 1, and faster than any card at Layer 2. Lightning delivers both - without compromising Bitcoin's core principles of decentralization and fixed supply.

🤔 Fun Facts & Curiosities
🌱 The Bitcoin Faucet
In 2010, developer Gavin Andresen created a website that gave away 5 BTC to anyone who visited - for free. The goal was to spread Bitcoin adoption. He gave away over 19,700 BTC total. At today's prices, each visitor's gift would be worth ~$500,000. The faucet ran until 2012, when 5 BTC was no longer pocket change.
🪙 Casascius Coins
Between 2011 and 2013, Mike Caldwell minted physical brass and silver coins with a Bitcoin private key hidden under a tamper-evident hologram on the back. Each coin was "loaded" with real BTC. They're now prized collector's items - a 1 BTC Casascius coin from 2011 has sold for over $100,000+ at auction (the physical coin plus the Bitcoin it holds). FinCEN shut down production in 2013.
😱 It's Not Exactly 21 Million
The real cap is 20,999,999.9769 BTC. Why? Several quirks: the genesis block's 50 BTC reward is unspendable (it was hardcoded, not a normal coinbase transaction). Some early miners accidentally claimed less than the full block reward due to bugs. And the halving math means the final satoshi is mined around year 2140, never quite reaching a clean 21 million. Close enough - but technically, never exactly.
💫 "HODL" Was a Typo
On December 18, 2013, a user named GameKyuubi posted on Bitcointalk: "I AM HODLING" - a drunken, typo-filled rant about why he wasn't selling during a crash. The misspelling stuck. "HODL" became Bitcoin's unofficial motto and was retroactively turned into a backronym: Hold On for Dear Life.
📡 "Running bitcoin" - The First Tweet
On January 11, 2009 - the day before receiving the first Bitcoin transaction - Hal Finney tweeted simply: "Running bitcoin". Two words. No hashtags, no hype. It might be the most understated announcement of a world-changing technology ever. Finney passed away in 2014 from ALS. He was cryopreserved - a fitting choice for someone who believed in the long arc of technology.
🛡️ The Lost Coins
An estimated 3-4 million BTC are permanently lost - forgotten hard drives, discarded laptops, lost passwords, deceased holders with no backup. James Howells of Wales famously threw away a hard drive with 8,000 BTC in 2013. He's been fighting his local council for permission to dig up the landfill ever since. The hard drive is still there. Somewhere.
🛰 Bitcoin From Space
Blockstream operates a satellite network that broadcasts the Bitcoin blockchain from space for free. Anyone with a small satellite dish can receive the full blockchain without an internet connection. It covers every continent. Bitcoin literally works even if the internet goes down in your region.
💻 The Value Overflow Bug
On August 15, 2010, someone exploited a bug to create 184 billion BTC out of thin air in block 74,638. Satoshi (still active at the time) deployed a fix within 5 hours. The bad block was orphaned and the bug was patched. It's the only time Bitcoin's supply was ever compromised, and it lasted less than a day.

Every "$1 = 1,309 BTC" and every lost hard drive is a reminder that we're still in the early chapters of this story. The protocol is 17 years old. The dollar is 250. Gold is 5,000. Bitcoin is just getting started.
