Bitcoin vs Tulip Mania

Is Bitcoin a tulip mania? Compare the 1637 bubble with 17 years of Bitcoin drawdowns and recoveries.

Open the interactive lesson →

"Bitcoin is just Tulip Mania" is the most common comparison critics make. It sounds clever. It makes the speaker seem historically literate. And it falls apart completely under even 30 seconds of analysis. Let's actually look at what happened in the Dutch Republic in the 1630s - and why Bitcoin is the exact opposite.

During the Dutch Golden Age, rare tulip bulbs became status symbols among the wealthy. A virus called the mosaic virus created unpredictable color patterns on petals, making certain bulbs uniquely beautiful - and uniquely scarce. Speculation exploded. At the peak in February 1637, a single Semper Augustus bulb sold for 10,000 guilders - enough to buy a luxury canal house in Amsterdam. Futures contracts traded on bulbs that hadn't even been dug up yet. Then on February 5, 1637, at a routine auction in Haarlem, nobody bid. The market collapsed virtually overnight. Prices fell 99% within weeks.

Tulips had no utility beyond beauty. They rotted. They could be grown by anyone with soil and patience - supply was theoretically infinite. The "rare" patterns from the mosaic virus could appear on any bulb unpredictably. There was no network. No protocol. No mathematical scarcity. No use case beyond "look at my flower." It was a pure speculative mania driven by social status and FOMO, with absolutely nothing underneath when sentiment shifted.

Infinite. Anyone can grow tulips. Bulbs multiply naturally every season. The "scarcity" was artificial and temporary.

Here's what's ironic: the people who compare Bitcoin to tulips are actually making an argument for Bitcoin, not against it. Tulip Mania teaches us that assets without mathematical scarcity, utility, and a decentralized network eventually collapse. Bitcoin has all three. It's the anti-tulip.

The comparison also reveals something else: the people making it have usually never read the whitepaper, never run a node, and never examined the monetary properties of Bitcoin. They heard someone on TV say "tulips" and repeated it. That's not analysis - that's a meme disguised as an argument.

Ask them: Can you send a tulip to someone in Nigeria in 10 minutes? Can you verify there will only ever be 21 million tulips? Can you store a tulip in your brain by memorizing 12 words? Can a tulip run 24/7 for 17 years with 99.99% uptime? No? Then it's not the same thing.
