For 5,000 years, civilizations have searched for the perfect money. Cowrie shells, salt, gold coins, paper bills - every attempt follows the same arc: it works brilliantly, then it fails spectacularly. We'll start at the very beginning and trace the full history of money to the present day.

You'll learn what makes "good money" good, why gold dominated for millennia, what happened when governments replaced it with paper promises in 1971, and why the dollar has lost over 85% of its purchasing power[1] since then (97% since the Federal Reserve was created in 1913). You'll see the graveyard of every fiat currency that came before it - and where the USD stands in that same timeline.

Sections include: The Nature of Money, Monetary Inflation, Full Faith & Credit, 1971: The Year Money Broke, Deflation vs Fiat, and The Fiat Graveyard.

Every currency in history shares one fatal flaw: someone can always create more of it. A king clips coins. A government fires up the printing press. A central bank types zeros into a computer. The result is always the same - the money in your pocket slowly becomes worthless.

Bitcoin is the first money in human history where no one can create more. Not a CEO. Not a president. Not an army. There will only ever be 21 million Bitcoin[2] - enforced by mathematics and a global network of computers, not by trust in any institution. It runs 24/7, its core protocol has never been broken into, it has never been shut down, and it has kept running with 99.98%+ uptime for 17+ years[4]. Very few banks, governments, or companies can make that claim.

Unlike fiat money - dollars, euros, yen - which can be printed in unlimited quantities, Bitcoin is digitally scarce. It can be sent anywhere on Earth - usually within an hour on the main chain, or in seconds for a fraction of a cent over the Lightning Network. When you hold your own keys, no bank or payment company can freeze or block it. And it doesn't require you to trust a bank, a broker, or a government. This course will show you exactly how it works, why it matters, and what it means for the future of money.
