🚨 The headline inflation number you see on the news is a lie. Not a small rounding error - a systematic, decades-long distortion designed to hide the true erosion of your purchasing power.

Every month, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index (CPI) , the official measure of inflation. Politicians point to it, central bankers target it, and your employer uses it to justify your annual raise (or lack thereof). In 2025, official CPI hovered around 2-3%. Sounds manageable, right? But the way CPI is calculated has been quietly, fundamentally changed - multiple times - always in the direction of making inflation look lower than it actually is.

The tricks are subtle but devastating. Substitution : when steak gets expensive, the government assumes you'll buy hamburger instead, and measures the cheaper item. Hedonic adjustments : your new phone costs $1,200 (same as last year), but the BLS says it's "really" cheaper because it has a better camera. Geometric weighting : a formula change in 1999 that automatically reduces the weight of things rising in price. If you measured inflation today the same way it was measured in 1980, the real rate would be closer to 8-12%.

Economist John Williams of ShadowStats has tracked this divergence for decades. Using the government's own 1980 methodology, he consistently finds real inflation running 5-8 points above the official CPI. Lower official inflation means lower Social Security adjustments, lower TIPS payouts, lower pressure on politicians. Meanwhile, asset prices have been inflating at 10-15% per year, invisible to the official number.

🏦 How the Federal Reserve Actually Creates Money

People say "the Fed prints money." But what does that actually mean? Here's the step-by-step process - dumbed down to its essence - of how brand-new dollars get created and flow into the economy. No jargon. No complexity. Just the truth.

Congress passes a budget that spends more than it collects in taxes. The difference is the deficit. To cover it, the U.S. Treasury issues bonds (IOUs) - essentially saying "lend us money now and we'll pay you back later with interest."

Here's where the magic trick happens. The Federal Reserve purchases Treasury bonds - but it doesn't use money it already has. It literally types new numbers into a computer. No vault is opened. No gold is moved. No taxpayer funds are used. The Fed creates dollars out of thin air and credits them to the Treasury's account. This is called Open Market Operations .

Now the Treasury has fresh money in its account. It sends it out: Social Security checks, military contracts, federal salaries, infrastructure projects, stimulus payments. These dollars flow to government employees, contractors, and citizens. This is the first wave - the money hits the economy.

When those dollars land in people's bank accounts, the banks don't just sit on them. Under fractional reserve banking , banks lend out the vast majority of every deposit. Your $1,000 paycheck gets deposited → the bank lends $900 of it to someone else → that $900 gets deposited at another bank → which lends $810 → and so on. One new dollar from the Fed can become $10 or more circulating in the economy. This is the money multiplier effect.

Think of it like a stone dropped in a pond. The first ripple (banks and government contractors) is the strongest - they get the new money first, before prices rise. They buy assets, invest, and spend at today's prices. The second ripple (large corporations, Wall Street) gets the money next, still at relatively low prices. The third ripple (small businesses, wages) arrives months or years later. By the time the outer ripple reaches you - the ordinary worker, saver, or retiree - prices have already risen. You get the same dollars but they buy less. The people furthest from the money printer always pay the price. Economists call this the Cantillon Effect .
