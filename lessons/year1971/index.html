1971: The Year Money Broke

One decision. Five decades of fallout. · ~6 min read

In 1971 the dollar lost its last link to gold. See what happened to wages, housing, and the wealth gap ever since. This is the reading version - the interactive lesson has the live demos, quizzes, and progress tracking.

Start the interactive lesson →

On Sunday evening, August 15, 1971, President Nixon interrupted the most popular show on television to announce that the United States would "suspend temporarily the convertibility of the dollar into gold." No vote. No debate. A 20-minute speech, and every currency on Earth became backed by nothing but a promise.

That "temporary" suspension is now in its sixth decade. And almost every chart that measures the financial health of an ordinary family bends in the wrong direction at the same spot. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

📈 The Great Divergence

For 25 years after WWII, when American workers produced more, they got paid more. Then the link snapped.[40]

Net productivity vs. hourly compensation of the typical US worker, inflation-adjusted. Source: Economic Policy Institute, BLS data.

Where did the difference go? Into asset prices. When money can be printed, the new money flows first to those who own things - stocks, real estate, businesses - and reaches wage earners last, after prices have already risen. Economists call it the Cantillon effect. Families call it "we both work and we're still behind."

🧮 The Bill: 1971 vs Today

The median family's income rose 8x since 1971. Everything that family actually needs rose faster.[41]

Healthcare in 1971 was ~7% of the economy. Today it is ~17.6%. Medical bills are now a leading cause of personal bankruptcy in America.

🏠 Are Houses Expensive, or Is Money Broken?

The median American house, 1971 to today. Same house. Two ways of measuring it.

Read that again. Priced in gold - the money the dollar used to be tied to - the median American house is cheaper than it was in 1971. Houses didn't get expensive. Cars didn't get expensive. College didn't get expensive. The ruler you measure them with got shorter, every single year.

📊 The Scoreboard Since 1971

Money is the unit society keeps score in. Break the unit, and the damage shows up far from Wall Street.[41][42]

Personal savings rate. In 1971 Americans saved 13 cents of every dollar. Saving stopped making sense when savings stopped holding value.

Odds of a 30-year-old out-earning their parents at the same age. A coin flip now. For Americans born in 1940, it was a near certainty.

Age 30 → Age 38

Median age of a first-time homebuyer. An entire decade of adulthood, gone to saving for a down payment that keeps running away.

Adult obesity. When real wages stall, the cheapest calories win - and the cheapest calories are processed. Food got cheap. Health got expensive.

Annual US suicides. 2023 set the all-time record. Financial stress is one of the most consistent predictors of despair.

CEO pay vs. the average worker. In 1965, the boss made 21 times what you did. Asset owners sit closest to the money printer.

Boomers at 35: ~21% of US wealth. Millennials at 35: ~7%.

Same age. Same country. One generation got sound-ish money and cheap houses. The other got the printer.

In 1971, one income bought a house, a car, and a family vacation. Today two incomes struggle to cover rent and daycare. That isn't because your grandparents worked harder than you. It's because every dollar they saved kept its promise, and every dollar you save quietly breaks it.

🎤 "The Cost of Our Inheritance"

At BTC Prague 2025, Strike CEO Jack Mallers stood in front of 10,000 people and described what it feels like to inherit this scoreboard: a generation financing its cars, degrees, and starter homes with debt; depression and anxiety at record levels; families cracking under money stress the way his own did.[42]

His argument was not that Bitcoin makes you rich. It was that a fixed supply of money is an ethical position: money nobody can print is money nobody can quietly take from you. He called Bitcoin a lifeboat - not from volatility, but from a system that taxes your time without asking.

⚖️ Is All of This Really Because of 1971?

Honest answer: no single switch explains fifty years of society. Oil shocks, globalization, automation, and demographics all pushed on these charts too, and economists argue about how much weight each one carries.

But notice what they don't argue about: after 1971, money could be created without limit, and it was. When savings can't hold value, people stop saving. When wages lag assets, workers fall behind owners. When the future gets more expensive every year, people borrow against it. You don't need a conspiracy. You just need bad money, and time.

This is the world Bitcoin was born into - and the genesis block makes it explicit. Satoshi's first act was to engrave a newspaper headline about bank bailouts into block zero. Bitcoin is the first money since 1971 with a supply no one can vote, print, or panic into existence. Whether it succeeds is an open question. What it responds to is not.
