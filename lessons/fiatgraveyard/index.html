The Fiat Graveyard

Every paper currency eventually returns to its intrinsic value: zero

Every purely faith-based currency in history has failed or been debased. Walk the graveyard, from imperial China to Venezuela. This is the reading version - the interactive lesson has the live demos, quizzes, and progress tracking.

Start the interactive lesson →

☠️ There have been over 775 fiat currencies in human history. Not a single one has survived. The average lifespan of a[30] fiat currency is 27 years. The US dollar, at 53 years off the gold standard, is living on borrowed time.

The word "fiat" means "by decree" - money that exists because a government says it does. It sounds modern, but governments have been running this experiment for centuries. And the result is always the same. Not sometimes. Not usually. Always. Every fiat currency ever created has either collapsed to zero, been replaced, or survived only by being devalued beyond recognition. There are no exceptions. Let's walk through the graveyard.

Chinese Jiaozi (1024-1160)

The world's first paper currency. Worked beautifully - until the Song Dynasty printed too much to fund wars. Hyperinflated, worthless within 140 years. Replaced by new currency. Same government repeated the cycle four more times across four dynasties.

French Assignat (1789-1796)

Revolutionary France printed paper money backed by seized church land. Within 7 years, it lost 99.97% of its value. It took a military dictator (Napoleon) to restore gold-backed money. The revolution ate itself through the printing press.

Weimar German Mark (1918-1923)

Germany printed money to pay WWI reparations. Prices doubled every 3.7 days at the peak.[6] A loaf of bread cost 200 billion marks. People burned cash for warmth because it was cheaper than firewood. A wheelbarrow of money couldn't buy a newspaper.

Hungarian Pengő (1927-1946)

The worst hyperinflation in recorded history. Prices doubled every 15 hours.[29] The government issued a 100 quintillion (1020) pengő note - the highest denomination banknote ever printed. The currency was replaced by the forint, backed temporarily by gold.

Zimbabwean Dollar (1980-2009)

Mugabe's government printed to fund wars and land seizures. Inflation hit 79.6 billion percent per month.[7] They printed a $100 trillion note. Eventually abandoned their currency entirely - Zimbabweans used US dollars and South African rand instead.

Venezuelan Bolívar (1879-present)

Still dying. Inflation exceeded 1,000,000% in 2018. The government has redenominated the currency three times, chopping zeros off - removing 14 zeros total. A burger costs millions of bolívares. Venezuelans have turned to Bitcoin in desperation.

Notice the pattern? It's always the same playbook: government faces crisis (war, debt, political failure) → prints money to paper over the problem → temporary relief → inflation accelerates → government prints more to compensate → spiral → collapse. Every single time. The crisis is different, the country is different, the century is different. The ending is identical.

The US dollar has lost 97% of its purchasing power since leaving gold. National debt exceeds $35 trillion.[8] The Fed's balance sheet ballooned from $900 billion (2008) to $9 trillion (2022).[9] 40% of all dollars ever created were printed in 2020[10]-2021. The dollar hasn't collapsed yet - but it's following the exact same trajectory as every currency that did. The question isn't if but when and how fast.

The defenders of the dollar will say "but the US is different - we have the world's reserve currency." So did the British pound, the Dutch guilder, the Spanish silver dollar, and the Roman denarius before them. Every reserve currency loses that status eventually. The average lifespan of a reserve currency is about 95 years. The dollar has been the reserve since 1944 - it's now 81 years in. History doesn't repeat, but it rhymes.

“It is entirely possible, of course, that something other than gold would be better as the basis for money. It’s just that, in over two thousand years, no one has been able to find it.”

- Edward Griffin, The Creature from Jekyll Island

Key terms

reserve currency
A currency held in significant quantities by governments and central banks worldwide as part of their foreign exchange reserves. The US dollar has been the world's reserve currency since the Bretton Woods agreement of 1944. No reserve currency has lasted forever - the average reign is about 95 years.
