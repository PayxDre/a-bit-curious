The Case Against Bitcoin

The strongest arguments AGAINST Bitcoin, presented at full strength: volatility, lost coins, energy, concentration, and state risk.

Open the interactive lesson →

Every other page on this site makes the case for Bitcoin. This one does the opposite, and it does it honestly. No strawmen, no "myths to debunk." These are the six strongest arguments against Bitcoin, presented the way a smart critic would present them. Some of them are partly right. If your conviction cannot survive this page, it was never conviction.

1. "It is far too volatile to be money"

"A currency that loses 75% of its value four separate times in fifteen years is not a store of value. It is a casino chip. Nobody prices a mortgage, a salary, or a pension in something that can halve in a quarter."

What the data says: This is simply true. Bitcoin fell roughly 93% in 2011, 85% in 2013-15, 84% in 2017-18, and 77% in 2021-22. Anyone who bought a cycle top waited two to four years just to break even. Volatility has declined as the asset has grown, and each drawdown has bottomed higher than the last, but Bitcoin today is still several times more volatile than gold or major currencies.

What remains unresolved: Whether volatility keeps declining until Bitcoin is stable enough to price bread in, or plateaus as a permanently violent asset. Seventeen years of data suggests the former. It does not prove it.

2. "One mistake and your money is gone forever"

"Banks have fraud departments, chargebacks, deposit insurance, and password resets. Bitcoin has none of these by design. Fat-finger an address, lose a seed phrase, fall for one phishing message, and there is no undo button and nobody to call."

What the data says: Also true, and the numbers are sobering. An estimated 3 to 4 million bitcoin, roughly a fifth of the supply, is considered lost forever - keys destroyed, seeds forgotten, owners deceased without backups. On top of that, crypto scams steal billions of dollars every year from real people. Self-custody transfers all responsibility to you, and most people have never held that kind of responsibility for anything.

What remains unresolved: Wallet design keeps improving - multisig, social recovery, inheritance tools - but "safe enough for your grandmother without help" does not exist yet. The scam drill later in this course exists precisely because this criticism is valid.

3. "It consumes a country's worth of electricity"

"Whatever mining apologists say about stranded energy and grid balancing, the absolute number keeps growing: Bitcoin consumes on the order of what the Netherlands consumes, to process a handful of transactions per second. A spreadsheet does not need a power plant."

What the data says: The Cambridge index estimates Bitcoin mining at roughly 100 to 180 TWh per year, genuinely comparable to a mid-size country. A meaningful and growing share is stranded, flared, or renewable energy, and miners do act as flexible grid load. But the honest framing is this: the energy is not an accident or an inefficiency. It IS the security. Bitcoin deliberately converts electricity into the cost of rewriting history.

What remains unresolved: Whether neutral, censorship-resistant money is worth a country's electricity bill is a values question, not a math question. This course thinks yes. Reasonable people conclude no. Both are looking at the same numbers.

4. "The whales own everything - you are exit liquidity"

"A tiny fraction of addresses controls most of the supply. Early adopters bought for pennies what you are asked to buy for a hundred thousand dollars. Meanwhile two mining pools frequently coordinate the majority of hashrate. Decentralized in theory, oligarchy in practice."

What the data says: Partly true, partly a measurement artifact. Address-level statistics wildly overstate concentration because one exchange address custodies coins for millions of customers. Adjusted analyses still show meaningful concentration, though it has trended downward every cycle as coins spread from early holders to new ones. Mining pool concentration is real, but pools are coalitions that miners can and do leave in hours when a pool misbehaves - it has happened.

What remains unresolved: Early adopters did get in cheap. Every monetary transition in history rewarded whoever showed up first, but "that is how adoption curves work" is an explanation, not a comfort.

5. "Governments will never allow a competitor to their money"

"The United States banned private gold ownership for forty-one years starting in 1933. When Bitcoin actually threatens monetary control, the response will not be an ETF approval. It will be exchange shutdowns, punitive exit taxes, and criminalized self-custody - coordinated across the G20."

What the data says: The precedent is real: Executive Order 6102 happened, and it targeted exactly the role Bitcoin wants to play. So far the observed pattern has run the other way - China banned Bitcoin repeatedly and the network shrugged; the US approved ETFs and holds seized bitcoin in reserve; nation-states now mine it. A ban cannot stop the network itself, but hostile coordination could strangle the on-ramps and crush the price for years.

What remains unresolved: "So far" is not "forever." The full-hostility scenario - a simultaneous G20 crackdown - has never been tested, and honest Bitcoiners admit it is the tail risk they cannot price.

6. "It produces nothing - the price is pure belief"

"A business earns profits. A bond pays coupons. A house collects rent. Bitcoin pays nothing and produces nothing. Its price rests entirely on the belief that someone else will value it later. If a generation simply loses interest, it does not crash dramatically - it just fades into irrelevance."

What the data says: Correct, and it applies equally to gold, which has carried monetary value on belief alone for five thousand years. Monetary goods are not businesses; their "yield" is salability across time and space. The honest version of this argument is not "no cash flows means worthless" - it is "monetary belief can die." It has died before, for seashells and for salt. Bitcoin's counter-evidence is seventeen years of survival through four 75%+ crashes, every obituary, and every ban.

What remains unresolved: Survival is evidence, not proof. Nobody can show you a theorem that Bitcoin must keep winning. Anyone who claims certainty, in either direction, is selling something.

🧠 Why a Bitcoin course argues against Bitcoin

Because education that hides the strongest counterarguments is not education, it is marketing. You should know exactly what you are accepting: real volatility, real responsibility, real political risk, and a monetary experiment that seventeen years of success has still not settled. The rule that follows from this page is simple and non-negotiable: never put money into Bitcoin that you cannot afford to watch drop 70% without flinching. If the case for Bitcoin is as strong as this course believes, it survives its critics at full strength. You just watched it try.
